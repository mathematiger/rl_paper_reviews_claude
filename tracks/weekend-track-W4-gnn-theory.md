## 2026-08-30 (Sunday) - Track W4: GNN theory with inspirational/deep theoretical background
**Title:** Understanding over-squashing and bottlenecks on graphs via curvature
**Authors:** Jake Topping, Francesco Di Giovanni, Benjamin Paul Chamberlain, Xiaowen Dong, Michael M. Bronstein
**Venue/Year:** ICLR 2022 (Oral; Outstanding Paper Honorable Mention); arXiv:2111.14522, November 2021 (University of Oxford / Imperial College London / Twitter Cortex)
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.14522 (OpenReview: https://openreview.net/forum?id=7UmjRGzp-A)

**Summary:** Message-passing GNNs fail on tasks needing long-range interaction, and the folklore explanation is "over-squashing": information from an exponentially growing r-hop neighbourhood is crushed into a fixed-width node vector, so distant nodes barely influence each other. Prior work (Alon & Yahav) diagnosed this qualitatively via graph bottlenecks; this paper makes it a geometric statement. The authors first formalise over-squashing analytically as the magnitude of the Jacobian dh_u^(r)/dx_v - the sensitivity of node u's representation after r layers to node v's input features - and show it is controlled by powers of the normalised adjacency matrix, so a topological property of the graph, not the network width, sets the ceiling. They then import Ricci curvature from Riemannian geometry, where negative curvature means geodesics diverge and volume grows exponentially - exactly the exponential-neighbourhood-growth condition behind over-squashing. Existing discrete curvatures (Ollivier, Forman) are either too expensive or too coarse, so they define Balanced Forman curvature, a cheap combinatorial edge quantity built from degrees, triangles and 4-cycles around an edge, and prove it lower-bounds Ollivier-Ricci curvature. The main theorems tie the two threads: sufficiently negatively curved edges provably induce a bottleneck that decays the Jacobian, and a positive curvature lower bound implies fast mixing and no over-squashing. The prescription follows from the geometry: run a discrete Ricci flow. Stochastic Discrete Ricci Flow (SDRF) repeatedly finds the most negatively curved edge, samples (softmax-tempered) an edge to add in its neighbourhood that maximally raises its curvature, and removes an edge above a positive-curvature threshold - flattening bottlenecks while roughly preserving edge count and topology. On node classification (Cornell, Texas, Wisconsin, Chameleon, Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed) SDRF beats the GCN baseline, k-NN rewiring and DIGL, with gains concentrated on the low-homophily graphs.

**Why this, why now:** This is the rare paper where a heavyweight piece of differential geometry is not decoration: the exponential neighbourhood growth that squashes messages *is* negative curvature, and once that identification is made, the fix - Ricci flow, the same object Perelman used on the Poincare conjecture - is handed to you by the analogy rather than invented. It also has the satisfying shape of a complete argument in one paper: define the failure mode analytically (Jacobian sensitivity), find the structural quantity that governs it (curvature), invent a computable version of it (Balanced Forman), prove the link, and derive the algorithm from the geometry instead of bolting one on.

**Connection to other papers:** It is the natural companion to "How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?" (logged 2026-08-16): Xu et al. bound what message passing can *distinguish* in one hop via Weisfeiler-Lehman, while this paper bounds what message passing can *transmit* across many hops via curvature - two independent ceilings on MPNNs, combinatorial and geometric, neither implied by the other. It gives the quantitative theory for the bottleneck phenomenon Alon & Yahav identified empirically in "On the Bottleneck of Graph Neural Networks and its Practical Implications" (ICLR 2021), and it opened the rewiring literature that followed - FoSR's spectral-gap rewiring and later work relating over-squashing to effective resistance are direct responses, replacing curvature with a spectral or resistance surrogate for the same bottleneck.

## 2026-08-16 (Sunday) - Track W4: GNN theory with inspirational/deep theoretical background
**Title:** How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?
**Authors:** Keyulu Xu, Weihua Hu, Jure Leskovec, Stefanie Jegelka
**Venue/Year:** International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR 2019); arXiv:1810.00826, October 2018
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826

**Summary:** Message-passing GNNs had achieved strong empirical results by 2018, but there was little theoretical understanding of what graph structures they can and cannot tell apart - practitioners picked aggregation functions (mean, max, sum) largely by trial and error. This paper builds a theoretical framework anchoring GNN expressiveness to the classical Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test: it proves that any message-passing GNN is at most as powerful as 1-WL at distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs, and derives the exact condition under which a GNN achieves this upper bound - the neighbor-aggregation function must be injective over multisets of neighbor features. Framed this way, mean- and max-pooling aggregators (as used in GCN and GraphSAGE) are provably unable to distinguish simple structures that differ only in neighbor multiplicity or distribution, while a sum aggregator composed with a suitable MLP can be. The paper turns this into a concrete architecture, the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN): sum-aggregate neighbor features, pass the result through an MLP, and combine with the previous node representation, iterated across layers with a WL-style multiset-hashing analogy. Empirically, GIN matches or exceeds prior GNN variants on graph classification benchmarks, closing much of the gap between theoretical expressive power and observed performance.

**Why this, why now:** A rare case of a deep theoretical argument - grounding neural graph representation learning in the decades-older combinatorial WL isomorphism test - that directly explains and predicts empirical architecture choices (sum vs. mean/max aggregation) rather than just describing them after the fact; it is the foundational reference point for the entire GNN-expressivity literature (over-smoothing, over-squashing, higher-order WL-GNNs) that followed it.
