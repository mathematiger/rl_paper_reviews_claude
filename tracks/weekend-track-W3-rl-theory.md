## 2026-08-29 (Saturday) - Track W3: RL theory with inspirational/deep theoretical background
**Title:** The Value Function Polytope in Reinforcement Learning
**Authors:** Robert Dadashi, Adrien Ali Taïga, Nicolas Le Roux, Dale Schuurmans, Marc G. Bellemare
**Venue/Year:** ICML 2019 (PMLR v97, pp. 1486-1495); arXiv:1901.11524, January 2019 (Google Brain / Mila)
**Link:** https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/dadashi19a.html (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.11524)

**Summary:** In a finite MDP with |S| states, every policy pi induces a value function V^pi = (I - gamma P^pi)^{-1} r^pi, a point in R^|S|. The paper asks a question that is obvious in hindsight and had gone essentially unanswered: what does the *set* of all attainable value functions V = {V^pi : pi in Pi} look like as a geometric object? The answer is that it is a polytope - a finite union of convex polytopes - and, strikingly, that it is in general *not* convex, and can even be self-intersecting. Two results do the work. The policy agreement lemma shows that fixing a policy's behaviour on k states confines the reachable value functions to (the intersection of V with) an affine subspace of dimension at most |S| - k. Pushing this to k = |S| - 1 gives the line theorem: if two policies agree everywhere except at a single state s, then as the action distribution at s is varied the value functions trace out a *line segment*, whose endpoints are attained by acting deterministically at s. Induction over states then generates the polytope, with vertices corresponding to deterministic policies, the boundary generated by policies deterministic in at least one state, and V* the unique vertex dominating all others. The authors then use the object as an instrument: low-dimensional MDPs let them *draw* the trajectories that value iteration, policy iteration, policy gradient and natural policy gradient carve through the polytope, exposing that these algorithms differ not just in rate but in whether they travel along the boundary or cut through the interior.

**Why this, why now:** A genuinely delightful result: an object every RL practitioner manipulates daily - the map from policies to value functions - turns out to have a crisp, drawable geometry that nobody had characterised, and the proof needs little more than the Neumann series for (I - gamma P^pi)^{-1} plus an induction on states. The non-convexity is the punchline worth sitting with, since so much intuition about policy optimisation quietly assumes a well-behaved landscape; and the resulting pictures of value iteration versus policy gradient are the rare theory artifact that immediately changes how you think about algorithms you already know.

**Connection to other papers:** Like Todorov's linearly-solvable MDPs (logged 2026-08-15), this is a "change the object you look at" paper - Todorov reparameterises control to make the Bellman equation linear, while Dadashi et al. leave the MDP untouched and instead characterise the geometry of what the standard operators are moving through, so the two are complementary rather than competing routes to structure. Its direct descendant is the companion paper "A Geometric Perspective on Optimal Representations for Reinforcement Learning" (Bellemare et al., 2019), which turns the polytope's vertices into adversarial value functions and uses them as a principled auxiliary-task objective for representation learning.

## 2026-08-15 (Saturday) - Track W3: RL theory with inspirational/deep theoretical background
**Title:** Linearly-Solvable Markov Decision Problems
**Authors:** Emanuel Todorov
**Venue/Year:** Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 19 (NIPS 2006)
**Link:** https://papers.nips.cc/paper/3002-linearly-solvable-markov-decision-problems

**Summary:** Standard MDPs are solved via the Bellman optimality equation, which is nonlinear because of the max/argmax over actions - this is exactly what forces iterative fixed-point solvers (value/policy iteration) instead of direct linear algebra, and what makes reusing solutions across related tasks hard. Todorov identifies a broad, useful subclass where this nonlinearity vanishes entirely. Instead of choosing discrete actions, the controller in a linearly-solvable MDP (LMDP) directly reshapes the transition probabilities of a given passive/uncontrolled Markov chain, paying a control cost equal to the KL divergence between controlled and uncontrolled next-state distributions. Because KL divergence is convex, the per-state minimization over next-state distributions admits a closed form; substituting it back into the Bellman equation removes the max altogether. Applying the exponential transform z = exp(-v/lambda) to the optimal value function then turns the Bellman equation into a genuinely linear eigenvector problem: the resulting "desirability function" z is the principal eigenvector of a matrix combining the uncontrolled dynamics with exponentiated state costs, solvable by standard linear algebra (e.g. power iteration) rather than dynamic programming. This yields an O(n) algorithm for shortest-path-like tasks via a largest-eigenvalue computation, and - because desirabilities combine linearly - lets solutions to elementary tasks be linearly recombined into solutions for new composite tasks without re-solving the MDP.

**Why this, why now:** One of the most elegant results in RL theory: reframing control as reshaping a Markov chain under a KL-divergence cost converts the notoriously nonlinear Bellman optimality equation into a linear eigenvector problem that is closed-form and compositional. It seeded an entire lineage - path-integral control, KL-control, and the control-as-inference view underlying maximum-entropy RL (e.g. soft actor-critic) - by showing that the "hard" nonlinearity in optimal control is an artifact of the action parameterization rather than something fundamental to the problem.
